Case Study

Building a Scalable Content Library at Toddle

How a fragmented knowledge base became a self-service content system, and what changed once the strategy shifted from adding more content to restructuring what already existed.

Real project, Toddle content library

Business Context

Toddle is an AI-powered teaching and learning platform used by K to 12 schools worldwide. As the platform evolved, so did the volume of learning resources, implementation guides, curriculum documentation, and product support content available to educators.

The challenge wasn't a lack of content. It was ensuring educators could quickly find the right information at the right stage of their journey. A fragmented knowledge experience created friction for users while increasing the effort required from customer-facing teams.

The Challenge

The objective was to create a content ecosystem that supported self-service learning, reduced information overload, and helped educators discover answers independently. This required thinking beyond individual articles and focusing on the overall content architecture.

The project needed to balance multiple priorities:

  • Make content easier to discover
  • Create a consistent structure across resources
  • Support product adoption through better information design
  • Align stakeholders across Product, Curriculum, Design, and Engineering
Key constraint

Multiple teams owned different parts of the content ecosystem, making it difficult to maintain consistency while balancing competing stakeholder priorities. Many educators were also initially hesitant to adopt the redesigned content library because they were already familiar with the existing experience. Driving adoption required not only improving the content architecture but also building confidence in the new navigation and workflows.

My Role

As Program Manager for the Content Library initiative, I was responsible for coordinating the development of a scalable content structure that improved how educational resources were organized and surfaced within the platform.

My work involved:

  • Collaborating with cross-functional stakeholders
  • Planning the overall content organization
  • Identifying opportunities to improve discoverability
  • Standardizing documentation approaches
  • Ensuring content decisions supported both user needs and business goals

Research, Insights & Strategic Pivot

Rather than approaching the project as a documentation exercise, I started by understanding how educators searched for information and where friction existed. This involved reviewing existing content organization, common educator workflows, internal stakeholder feedback, product requirements, curriculum dependencies, and cross-functional implementation needs.

One key observation emerged: users don't think in terms of internal product teams, they think in terms of the task they're trying to accomplish. The content architecture needed to reflect that mental model.

What changed

Initially, the focus was on expanding the content library. Research and stakeholder feedback revealed that the primary issue wasn't a lack of content, but poor discoverability. The strategy shifted from creating more resources to restructuring existing knowledge through improved information architecture, standardized documentation, and user journey based navigation.

Cross-functional Collaboration

The success of the project depended on close collaboration across multiple teams.

  • Product teams provided feature context
  • Curriculum specialists ensured educational accuracy
  • Designers improved usability
  • Engineering enabled implementation
  • Customer-facing teams shared recurring user questions

This collaboration helped ensure that content decisions aligned with both product functionality and educator needs.

Key Strategic Decisions

Prioritizing information architecture over content volume

Rather than producing additional documentation immediately, I focused on improving how existing knowledge was structured. Better organization would create greater long-term value than simply publishing more content.

Designing for self-service

The objective wasn't just to answer questions, it was to help educators find answers independently, reducing friction throughout onboarding and ongoing product use.

Building repeatable systems

Instead of solving isolated content problems, the project established a framework that could scale as the product and resource library continued to grow.

Reflection

Looking back, this project fundamentally changed how I think about content strategy.

Effective content isn't created by publishing more. It's created by designing systems that make knowledge easier to discover, understand, and use.

That same principle now shapes how I approach AI Search Optimization. Whether the audience is a teacher using a product, a buyer using Google, or someone asking ChatGPT a question, the goal remains the same: structure information so the right answer can be found with minimal effort.

Key Takeaways

  • Content strategy begins with information architecture, not writing.
  • Cross-functional collaboration produces stronger content systems.
  • Discoverability is as important as content quality.
  • Scalable frameworks outperform one-off content creation.
  • Great content strategy helps both people and machines find answers faster.
