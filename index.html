01

Who I am as a strategist

A brief introduction to my background and philosophy

I come to content strategy from a background of managing complex, cross-functional delivery — the kind of work where you're translating what a business needs into a plan multiple teams can actually execute against, and then defending that plan when the data says to change course. That instinct is exactly what I bring to content: treating it as a system to be architected, not a queue of pieces to be produced.

My interest in SEO and content strategy has increasingly centered on one shift: AI answer engines have changed who the real audience is. A page no longer only needs to persuade a human reader — it needs to be structured clearly enough that an AI system can extract it, trust it, and cite it correctly. I care about content strategy that holds up under both of those readers at once: the person who lands on the page, and the system that decides whether they ever needed to.

The sections below walk through how I'd approach that work in practice — from the framework I use to plan strategy, through a sample project, to how I'd measure whether any of it actually worked.

02

How I approach content strategy

A five-stage framework, not a task list

  1. Understand the business goal, not the content request

    Before any topic list exists, I want to know what business outcome content is actually meant to move — pipeline, retention, category authority — because that goal determines what "good" content even means for this account.

  2. Audience and search-behavior research

    Mapping who the buyer is, what they ask a search engine versus what they ask an AI assistant, and where those two behaviors diverge. This is where I decide which content needs to win a ranking versus win a citation.

  3. Content architecture and planning

    Turning research into a structure: topic clusters, entity relationships, and a prioritized roadmap — the calendar comes later, this is the skeleton it hangs on.

  4. Execution with dual optimization

    Every piece is built to satisfy both a traditional ranking algorithm and an AI extraction pass — clear entity definitions, scannable structure, and schema where it earns its place.

  5. Measurement and iteration

    Reviewing what actually moved — traffic, citations, conversions — and being willing to kill or redirect a plan that isn't earning its budget, rather than defending it out of sunk cost.

03

A strategy I built and evolved

Sample project, built to demonstrate strategic thinking
Illustrative sample — not a real client engagement

The business problem

Brightleaf Analytics, a fictional B2B SaaS company, was losing organic pipeline even as its keyword rankings held steady. The pattern: traffic was flat, but demo requests attributed to organic content had dropped over two quarters.

Research

A content audit showed most cornerstone pages were written and structured for keyword ranking — long intros, dense paragraphs, no clear answer up front — with almost nothing formatted in a way an AI answer engine could confidently extract and cite. Buyers had started using AI tools earlier in their research, and Brightleaf's content simply wasn't showing up there.

Strategy pivot point

The strategy shifted from "rank for more keywords" to "become the cited source for the category's core questions." That meant consolidating overlapping pages into clear topic clusters, rewriting cornerstone content to lead with direct answers, and treating citation potential as a planning input alongside search volume — not an afterthought.

Execution

Rebuilt the top 12 cornerstone pages around question-based H2s and direct-answer openings, added FAQ schema where content was genuinely structured as Q&A, and built an internal linking backbone connecting cluster pages back to a small set of pillar pages.

Results illustrative figures

Over the following two quarters (illustrative): organic-attributed demo requests recovered and grew roughly 22%, average time-to-first-citation in AI-generated answers for tracked queries improved, and three previously flat pillar pages began appearing consistently in AI Overview responses for their target questions.

The real pivot wasn't the rewrite — it was changing what we measured as "working" before deciding anything needed to change.
04

Preparing content for AI-powered discovery

SEO and AEO, treated as one discipline

Entity optimization

Naming people, products, and concepts clearly and consistently, and linking them to authoritative external context, so AI systems can confidently identify what a page is actually about.

Schema markup

Structured data — especially FAQPage — used only where the content genuinely matches the format, mapping directly to how people phrase questions to AI tools.

Topical authority

Building clusters that fully cover a subject's question tree, not single pages hoping to rank for one keyword in isolation.

Citation potential

Writing direct, extractable answers near the top of a section — content that reads correctly even when quoted alone, out of context, by an AI system.

Structured content & FAQs

Clear heading hierarchy, scannable formatting, and genuine FAQ sections that anticipate the exact questions a buyer would type into an AI assistant.

E-E-A-T principles

Demonstrating real experience and expertise in the content itself — credible sourcing, named authorship, and specificity — since AI systems increasingly weigh trust signals when choosing what to cite.

06

How great content gets produced

Editorial workflow, and where AI tools fit

  1. Ideation & research

    Topic sourced from strategy roadmap, validated against real search and AI-query behavior. AI tools help synthesize research fast; the topic call itself stays human.

  2. Brief & drafting

    A structured brief — audience, intent, target entities, question set — before drafting starts. AI tools accelerate first-draft structure; judgment on argument and nuance stays human.

  3. SEO & AEO optimization pass

    Headings, schema candidates, and entity clarity reviewed against both traditional SEO and citation-readiness criteria.

  4. Stakeholder review

    Business and subject-matter stakeholders review for accuracy and positioning — the point where a strategist defends or adjusts the plan based on real pushback.

  5. Publish

    Content goes live with schema, internal links, and metadata fully in place — not added later as an afterthought.

  6. Post-publication monitoring

    Tracking rankings, AI citation appearances, and engagement, then feeding what's learned back into the next round of planning.

After

What is a content strategy framework? A content strategy framework is the repeatable process a team uses to turn business goals into published content — covering audience research, planning, execution, and measurement. Most frameworks fail at one of two points: skipping audience research, or never returning to measurement to adjust the plan.

Why each change was made

The original opens with three sentences of throat-clearing before saying anything — unusable by an AI system looking to extract a direct answer, and a slow start for a human reader too. The rewrite leads with the exact question a reader (or an AI system) would ask, answers it in the first sentence, and immediately adds a specific, useful detail rather than a vague promise of value later in the article. Clarity and citation-readiness improved together, not as a trade-off.

Traffic and rankings tell me if content is findable. Engagement and conversion tell me if it's useful once found. All four get reviewed together before I call a strategy "working."

09

Continuous improvement as a strategist

What I've learned, and where I think this is headed

The biggest shift in how I think about content strategy hasn't been a new tactic — it's a change in who counts as "the reader." For years, content strategy optimized entirely for a human decision at the end of a search. Now a meaningful share of that decision-making happens inside an AI system before a human ever lands on a page, which means structure and trustworthiness have become strategic decisions, not formatting details.

The clearest lesson from working through that shift: measuring the wrong thing for too long is more dangerous than any single bad piece of content. A strategy can look healthy on rankings alone while quietly losing the audience that matters most. Being willing to change what "success" means, and say so plainly to stakeholders, is a harder skill than any individual piece of execution.

Where I think this is headed: content strategy and technical SEO are going to keep merging with something closer to structured-data engineering. The strategists who stay valuable will be the ones fluent in both the story and the schema.
